# Portfolio Website Content

Build the portfolio around evidence of capability, not decoration. Optimize for a visitor who wants to understand who the person is, what they can do, and whether their work is worth exploring further.

## Required content

Include or deliberately account for these elements when relevant:

- **Identity and direction:** name, current role or professional identity, and a concise statement of the kind of work the person does or wants to do.
- **Selected work:** a small set of strong projects rather than an exhaustive dump of everything ever built.
- **About:** enough context to understand interests, strengths, background, and direction without repeating the resume verbatim.
- **Professional evidence:** experience, education, research, awards, publications, leadership, certifications, or other evidence that matters for this person's target roles.
- **Contact and external profiles:** a clear way to reach the person and links to relevant destinations such as GitHub, LinkedIn, resume, publications, or demos.

Not every portfolio needs every category as a separate section. Combine or omit weak sections when the underlying information is represented more effectively elsewhere.

## Project presentation

For each featured project, communicate the useful story rather than only listing a title and technology stack.

Capture, when available:

1. **What it is** — one concise description.
2. **Why it exists** — the problem, need, research question, or motivation.
3. **What the person did** — their contribution, especially for team projects.
4. **How it was built** — technologies and important engineering choices, without turning the page into a dependency list.
5. **What was difficult or notable** — meaningful technical, product, research, or design challenges.
6. **What happened** — measurable results, users, performance improvements, findings, adoption, completion status, or lessons where truthful and available.
7. **Where to inspect it** — repository, live demo, paper, video, case study, or other relevant link.

Prefer concrete evidence over self-evaluative claims such as "highly skilled," "innovative," or "expert."

## Skills and technologies

- Let projects and experience demonstrate skills whenever possible.
- Use a compact technology/skills summary only when it helps scanning.
- Avoid arbitrary proficiency bars, percentages, star ratings, or unsupported labels such as "90% Python."
- Group technologies meaningfully if a long flat list would obscure the important ones.

## Content quality

- Write for fast scanning first and deeper exploration second.
- Put the most decision-relevant information early.
- Prefer specific nouns and outcomes over generic adjectives.
- Keep descriptions concise enough that strong projects remain distinguishable from one another.
- Preserve the person's actual voice and facts.
- Never invent employers, metrics, project impact, technologies, awards, dates, responsibilities, or outcomes. If important information is missing, ask for it or use an explicit placeholder rather than fabricating it.
- Avoid filler sections that exist only because portfolio templates commonly include them.

## Audience check

Before finalizing the portfolio content, verify that a recruiter, hiring manager, collaborator, or technical reviewer can quickly answer:

- Who is this person?
- What kind of opportunities or work are they oriented toward?
- What have they actually built, researched, led, or contributed to?
- Which examples best demonstrate their capabilities?
- What was their individual contribution?
- Is there evidence behind the claims?
- How can I inspect more work, view a resume, or contact them?

If these answers are difficult to find, improve the content structure before adding more material.

## Boundary with UI/UX

Do not prescribe a visual aesthetic, color palette, typography system, animation style, layout trend, or brand personality through this skill. Those decisions are personal and should come from the user's own UI/UX skill or direct instructions.
